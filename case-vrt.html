A family garden centre with three greenhouses, a paper stock list, and a phone that rang all spring with the same four questions.

01 — The problem

The catalogue lived in a binder

Everything they sold was written down by hand and re-typed into a spreadsheet each season. Customers rang to ask whether something was in stock, what it cost, and whether it could be delivered to their village — four hundred calls a month, all answered by the owner while carrying trays. They did not need a beautiful website. They needed the binder to answer the phone.

02 — The approach

What we decided to do about it.

Data first

Import before design

We got the spreadsheet cleaned and imported on day two, before a single page was designed. Designing a shop with real product names and real photo gaps shows you the actual problems.

Delivery

Postcode zones, not guesswork

Three delivery rings priced by distance, with a free-collection option that turned out to be the most-used choice by a wide margin.

Handover

Built so they can run it

Product editing was reduced to five fields. If a field was not going to be filled in every week, it was hidden from the admin screen.

03 — Zero to live

The whole build, in order.

Days 1–2

Scope and catalogue audit

We went through the binder together. 340 products became 210 after merging variants — the first real win, and it happened before any design.

Days 3–4

Import and taxonomy

Categories, seasonal tags, stock levels, and a photo-gap list handed back to them so they could shoot the missing 60 items themselves.

Days 5–8

Storefront build

Category and product pages, cart, checkout, delivery zones, Stripe and cash-on-delivery. Staging link live from day five.

Day 9

Training

Two hours in their office, screen recorded so the seasonal staff can rewatch it. They added four products themselves before we left.

Days 10–11

Launch

Speed pass, Google Business linked, first orders monitored live. First online sale came in on the second day.

04 — Two calls worth explaining

Where we pushed back.

No account required to buy

WooCommerce pushes registration hard. Their customers are over fifty and buying soil — an account is a wall. Guest checkout only, with an optional 'save my address' box at the end. Cart abandonment sat at 18%, well under the retail norm.

[ checkout flow — 1600×900 ]

Stock is honest, not optimistic

We showed real counts rather than a generic 'in stock' badge. The owner was nervous it would look sparse. It did the opposite — 'only 4 left' on seasonal bulbs became their best-performing element and cut the stock-check phone calls almost entirely.
